# Building Trustworthy, Actionable AI Teaching Recommendations for alcoia

## TL;DR
- **Ship a diagnostic-first, non-directive recommendation layer.** The strongest evidence (Holstein/McLaren/Aleven's Lumilo RCT: 286 students, 8 teachers, real-time analytics improved learning at r≈0.21 vs. business-as-usual) shows analytics change teaching when they are *actionable in the moment*, while a planning-only dashboard (Xhakaj et al. Luna) changed teacher knowledge and lesson plans but produced NO confirmed learning gain. Teachers consistently reject prescriptive automated judgment of their teaching but accept interpretable evidence that preserves their autonomy.
- **The single highest-value capability is correctly classifying the TYPE of failure**, because each type has a different, research-backed response: a genuine misconception needs refutation (Hedges' g≈0.41 for refutation text; g≈0.93–1.10 for broader conceptual-change strategies), difficulty needs worked examples/scaffolding, disengagement needs redesign, and overload needs cognitive-load reduction. Re-explaining a confident misconception is the documented failure mode — but note the counterintuitive "hypercorrection effect": high-confidence errors are actually *easier* to correct once directly confronted with feedback.
- **Ground every recommendation in the specific data that triggered it, quote the evidence, gate it behind confidence thresholds, and show raw student answers.** LLM diagnosis of *why* students err is unreliable (diagnostic accuracy 0.44–0.67 in code-explanation studies; classifiers miss >40% of correct-answer misconceptions; hallucinated diagnoses documented), so the layer must express uncertainty, cite its evidence, and let the instructor override — the exact features that overcome algorithm aversion.

## Key Findings

**1. Dashboards that merely display data rarely change teaching; actionability and timing are decisive.** Jivet et al. (2017, "Awareness Is Not Enough," EC-TEL) found most learning-analytics dashboards stop at awareness and lack the reference frames and pedagogical grounding needed to drive action — indeed, they warned current designs often "foster competition between learners rather than knowledge mastery." Matcha et al. (2020, IEEE Transactions on Learning Technologies 13(2):226–245) reviewed empirical dashboard studies from a self-regulated-learning perspective and found few are grounded in learning theory or shown to change behavior. The Carnegie Mellon contrast is the clearest natural experiment. Xhakaj, Aleven & McLaren (2017, EC-TEL, LNCS 10474; quasi-experimental study, 5 middle-school teachers, 17 classes) showed a planning-oriented ITS dashboard (Luna) changed the causal chain — "Luna influences what teachers know about their students' learning in the ITS and that the teachers' updated knowledge affects the lesson plan they prepare, which in turn guides what they cover in a class session" — with teachers focusing "primarily on dashboard information about the challenges their students were experiencing." But the same paper states plainly: "Results did not confirm that Luna increased student learning." By contrast, Holstein, McLaren & Aleven's real-time mixed-reality tool Lumilo (2018, AIED, LNCS 10947; 3-condition experiment, 286 students, 18 classrooms, 8 teachers) found presenting teachers with real-time analytics "had a positive impact on student learning, compared with both business-as-usual and classroom monitoring support without advanced analytics" (r≈0.21 vs. control; r≈0.11 vs. glasses-without-analytics, per the npj Science of Learning 2025 review). Follow-up (Karumbaiah et al. 2023, N=173) tied the "deep-dive" diagnostic look-up specifically to learning gains (β=0.19, 95% CI [0.07, 0.31], p=.001, +3.1% variance explained). **The lesson for alcoia: the diagnostic detail that lets a teacher act — not the summary metric — is what moves outcomes.**

**2. Teachers want diagnostic information they interpret, not prescriptions.** In Holstein et al.'s co-design work (2019, Journal of Learning Analytics 6(2):27–52; 10 middle-school teachers across 5 schools), teachers rated seeing student "thought processes" *above* automated skill-mastery percentages, which they explicitly called "less actionable"; the "knowing whether students really know something" superpower "ranked lower than most other common superpower ideas." Their AIED 2019 participatory-design study ("Designing for Complementarity," 24 participants — 10 teachers, 14 students) explicitly favored a system that helps teachers decide "but without strongly suggesting a particular course of action," and teachers resented tools that appeared to judge their teaching (one reacting to a movement-tracking system: "It's just too much in my business now. You better be quiet and give me a break"). This aligns with algorithm-aversion research: Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey (2015, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General 144(1):114–126, five studies) showed "people are especially averse to algorithmic forecasters after seeing them perform, even when they see them outperform a human forecaster... they more quickly lose confidence in algorithmic than human forecasters after seeing them make the same mistake," and aversion is *more* pronounced among domain experts (Logg et al.; Jussupow et al.). The best-documented antidote: Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey (2018, "Overcoming Algorithm Aversion," Management Science 64(3):1155–1170) found letting people even slightly modify an algorithm's output substantially increased use — the effect held "even when participants were severely restricted in the modifications they could make."

**3. The four failure types and their divergent, evidence-based responses.** This is the pedagogical core the AI must encode:
- **(a) Confident misconception** (high confidence + wrong, clustered on one distractor): needs *refutation*, not re-explanation. Refutation-text meta-analysis (Schroeder & Kucera 2022, Educational Psychology Review 34(2):957–987): "Analysis of 44 independent comparisons (n = 3,869) showed that refutation text is associated with a positive, moderate effect (g = 0.41, p < .001) compared to other learning conditions. This effect was consistent and robust across a wide variety of contexts." A larger educational-psychology meta-analysis (2025, Educational Psychologist 60(1); 76 studies, 111 samples, 294 effect sizes) confirmed a consistent advantage of refutation over non-refutation text. Broader conceptual-change strategies (cognitive conflict, cognitive bridging, ontological shift) show larger effects — Pacaci et al. (2024, Journal of Research in Science Teaching 61(6):1263–1325; 218 studies, 18,051 students) found overall g=1.10 (95% CI [1.01, 1.19]; robust Bayesian adjusted g=0.93), though with high heterogeneity (prediction interval [0.19, 2.38]). Crucially, the "hypercorrection effect" (Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001; Metcalfe et al. 2012) shows high-confidence errors are corrected *more* readily than low-confidence ones when learners get explicit, surprising feedback that co-activates the misconception and the correct concept — so "confidently wrong" is an opportunity, not a dead end. (Van Loon et al. found refutation texts specifically improved correction of high-confidence misconceptions.)
- **(b) Genuine difficulty/effortful material** (low confidence + wrong, dispersed answers): needs worked examples and faded scaffolding. The worked-example effect (Sweller & Cooper 1985) is among the most replicated findings in educational psychology; effective for novices, with guidance faded as competence grows (Renkl & Atkinson).
- **(c) Disengagement/abandonment** (drop-off, non-completion): needs redesign of the reading task — chunking, relevance, motivation — not more content on the same concept.
- **(d) Cognitive overload** (struggle concentrated in dense passages, high effort across many items): needs load reduction — segmenting, removing extraneous load, worked examples to free working memory.

**4. Misconceptions are cataloguable enough to detect — from *patterns*, not single answers.** Distractor analysis and IRT-based methods (Sadler's 1998 Project STAR, the first to use IRT to model misconceptions and which showed the probability of choosing a wrong answer does not always fall as ability rises; multidimensional Bayesian IRT for discovering misconceptions from concept-test data; topic modeling of constructed responses that recovered 22 distinct misconception clusters from 1,515 biology responses) reliably surface shared misconceptions from wrong-answer clustering. Research-designed concept inventories (e.g., the Force Concept Inventory) built distractors directly from documented misconceptions. This supports alcoia's "confidently wrong" signal — but detection depends on *aggregate clustering on specific distractors*, not individual answers, reinforcing the 5-student cohort floor.

**5. LLM diagnosis of the *reason* for errors is unreliable and must be safeguarded.** Code-explanation studies (Can LLMs Identify Gaps and Misconceptions, 2025) show diagnostic accuracy of 0.44–0.67 (best after fine-tuning/ORPO). A 2026 study ("The Correct Answer Trap") found fine-tuned classifiers miss over 40% of "correct-answer" misconceptions and that reasoning models introduce false-positive rates "that make standalone classification impractical at classroom prevalence." GPT-4 has been documented producing "an incorrect, hallucinated diagnosis of a common misconception." LLM tutoring agents "systematically over-rejected valid but suboptimal reasoning and over-validated incorrect solutions, precisely where adaptive tutoring matters most." LLM lesson-plan generation suffers a documented "confidence conundrum" (confident regardless of actual certainty) and struggles with "high-inferential criteria, such as the depth of pedagogical reasoning." Implication: the LLM should *classify failure type and surface evidence*, not autonomously assert deep causal diagnoses.

## Details

**What makes recommendations get used.** Formative assessment that feeds back into instruction is high-leverage: Black & Wiliam's "Inside the Black Box" (Phi Delta Kappan 80(2), October 1998, reviewing ~580 articles) reported "typical effect sizes of the formative assessment experiments were between 0.4 and 0.7... larger than most of those found for educational interventions," with the largest benefit for lower attainers (an effect size of 0.7 would raise a mid-pack nation like the U.S. into the top five internationally). But the mechanism requires teachers to *act on* the information; teacher data literacy (Mandinach & Gummer 2016) and structured sense-making time are prerequisites (Swiss survey, N=1,059, found data literacy the central predictor of digital data use; an instructor-use study showed analytics-supported inquiry succeeds specifically when paired with dedicated sense-making consultation). Actionability research (Journal of Learning Analytics, "What Constitutes an Actionable Insight") stresses recommendations must be specific, tied to a decision, and low-effort to act on. Generic advice teachers dismiss; advice tied to *this passage, this cohort, this misconception* gets used.

**Encoding the pedagogy — the confidence-accuracy quadrants.** The AI's decision logic should map the four quadrants to responses:
- **High confidence + correct** → mastered; move on.
- **High confidence + wrong (clustered on one distractor)** → *shared misconception* → refutation approach: state the misconception explicitly, contrast it with the correct concept, explain why the intuition fails — leveraging hypercorrection.
- **Low confidence + wrong** → difficulty → worked examples; elaborative interrogation (Dunlosky et al. 2013 "moderate utility," meta-analytic d≈0.56) and self-explanation (d≈0.54) as instructor-deployable prompts.
- **Low confidence + correct** → fragile knowledge → retrieval/practice testing (Dunlosky "high utility," d≈0.74) to consolidate before moving on.

Peer instruction (Mazur) and just-in-time teaching (Novak) are the classic instructor moves once you can see what students didn't understand. Crouch & Mazur (2001, American Journal of Physics 69(9):970–977) documented Mazur's Harvard course rising from a normalized Force Concept Inventory gain of 0.25 under traditional lecture to 0.49 in the first semester of peer instruction, with gains of 49–74% over ten years "consistently improving over time as more reformed elements are added." This sits against Hake's (1998, American Journal of Physics 66(1):64–74, N=6,542) baseline: 14 traditional courses averaged normalized gain ⟨g⟩=0.23±0.04 versus 48 interactive-engagement courses at ⟨g⟩=0.48±0.14. These are exactly the "here's what to do next class" recommendations alcoia can surface. Threshold concepts (Meyer & Land 2003) and "troublesome knowledge" (counter-intuitive, alien, conceptually difficult) explain *why* certain passages recur as trouble spots across cohorts — alcoia's trouble map is effectively a threshold-concept detector.

**Critiques to encode as caveats.** The Posner/Strike (1982) conceptual-change model was critiqued as "cold" (Pintrich, Marx & Boyle 1993) for treating learning as purely rational and ignoring motivation and affect; cognitive conflict alone often fails in real classrooms because making the conflict *meaningful* depends on prior knowledge, epistemological beliefs, and engagement (Limón 2001). So the AI should not promise that refutation "fixes" a misconception — it should frame it as the *appropriate* strategy with known limits, requiring follow-up retrieval (hypercorrected high-confidence errors tend to return over a delay unless a subsequent test intervenes; Butler, Fazio & Marsh 2011).

**Communicating uncertainty without being useless.** The layer should: (1) state the evidence ("18 of 22 students who answered chose distractor C, and 15 rated ≥4/5 confidence"); (2) name the inferred failure type with a hedge scaled to evidence strength; (3) offer the strategy as a suggestion, not a directive; (4) always show raw answers so instructors can "second guess the system" — a documented, valued Lumilo behavior where teachers used concrete raw examples to infer deeper causes; (5) respect the 5-student cohort floor. Transparency of reasoning and override capability are the two best-supported levers against expert algorithm aversion.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Ship diagnostic classification, not prescription.** For each trouble spot, output: the failure type (misconception / difficulty / disengagement / overload), the specific evidence (answer distribution, confidence distribution, abandonment point, cited passage), and a *suggested* strategy with a one-line rationale. Frame everything as input to the instructor's judgment. **Threshold to change course:** if instructors report the tool "judges" them or systematically ignore recommendations, strip prescriptive language further and move toward pure evidence surfacing.

**Stage 2 — Gate recommendations behind evidence thresholds.** Only assert "shared misconception" when wrong answers cluster on one distractor (e.g., ≥50% of errors on one option) AND confidence is high AND n≥5. Below thresholds, downgrade to "possible difficulty — worth checking." Never let the LLM assert a causal diagnosis the data cannot support; use it to draft language *around* the statistics, not to invent the statistics. Given documented LLM diagnostic accuracy of only ~0.44–0.67, treat any LLM-generated "reason" as a hypothesis for the instructor, not a finding.

**Stage 3 — Make strategies concrete and disciplinary.** Instead of "re-teach section 4," output: "Students hold [specific misconception]. Re-explaining rarely works because they don't perceive a gap; try a refutation — state the misconception, show why it's intuitive, then contrast with [correct concept]. Because they answered confidently, direct confrontation is likely to stick (hypercorrection effect) — then follow with a retrieval question next class to prevent the error returning." Pair with ready-made peer-instruction/JiTT prompts drawn from the actual failed questions.

**Stage 4 — Always show the raw evidence and enable override/feedback.** Let instructors expand any recommendation to see the underlying answers and confidence ratings, mark it wrong, and dismiss it. This both improves the model and is the single best-documented antidote to algorithm aversion in expert users (Dietvorst et al. 2018).

**Stage 5 — Frame the whole product as helping the teacher, never evaluating them.** Given documented teacher fears of surveillance and performance evaluation (and evidence that ethical/surveillance concerns are concrete barriers to adoption, not abstract worries), the recommendation layer must never imply the *instructor* failed. Language should center student comprehension and next steps, not teaching quality. Keep "a layer of human mediation" explicit in the UI.

**Highest-value recommendation types, ranked:** (1) confident-misconception alerts with a refutation script — highest value because they catch the failure teachers are least likely to detect unaided and prescribe the one strategy that works; (2) "fragile knowledge" flags (low-confidence-correct) prompting retrieval practice — high value, easy action, strong effect size; (3) abandonment/redesign flags — high value for course revision; (4) overload flags — valuable but hardest to distinguish from difficulty, so hedge most heavily.

## Caveats
- The strongest learning-gain evidence (Lumilo, r≈0.21) is from real-time K-12 ITS classrooms; alcoia's asynchronous reading-comprehension context is closer to the Luna dashboard, which changed practice but showed *no confirmed learning gain* — so alcoia should not over-claim outcome effects without its own controlled evaluation.
- Refutation and conceptual-change effect sizes come largely from science education and partly from lab studies; transfer to arbitrary reading material is plausible but unproven, and heterogeneity is high (Pacaci et al.'s prediction interval spans 0.19 to 2.38).
- LLM diagnostic accuracy is a live risk; the design deliberately leans on statistics plus human judgment precisely because LLM causal diagnosis is unreliable and prone to confident hallucination.
- The prescriptive-vs-diagnostic question is genuinely contested: some researchers (e.g., Dazo et al.; Kasepalu et al.; the CADA authors) argue dashboards are too *minimally* actionable and should offer more concrete hints. The resolution adopted here — actionable but non-directive — is a reasoned judgment call, not settled consensus.
- Effect sizes cited (Hedges' g, Cohen's d, correlation r, normalized gain) are not directly comparable across metrics and studies; treat them as magnitude indicators, not precise predictions for alcoia's context.
- Dunlosky et al.'s technique ratings (self-explanation, elaborative interrogation rated "moderate utility") reflect that these techniques were, as of that review, under-tested in authentic classroom settings — a reason to present them as options rather than guarantees.
