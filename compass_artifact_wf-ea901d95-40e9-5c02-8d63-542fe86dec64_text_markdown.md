# Retrieval Practice, Comprehension, and the Design of alcoia: An Evidence Review

## TL;DR
- **Retrieval practice reliably improves memory and, more weakly, transfer — but it does not manufacture understanding that was never there.** The testing effect is among the most robust findings in learning science (practice testing vs. restudy g ≈ 0.51; vs. no activity g ≈ 0.93), and it *does* extend to inference and application (transfer d ≈ 0.40). But its benefit is gated by retrieval success: when a learner cannot retrieve because they never encoded/understood the material, testing *without feedback* yields essentially nothing (the bifurcation model). For a reader whose comprehension is breaking down, retrieval questions must be paired with feedback and, when comprehension has genuinely failed, with re-encoding scaffolds — not retrieval alone.
- **The single most actionable finding is that VARIED retrieval beats repeated identical retrieval for transfer.** Asking the same underlying knowledge from different angles (definition → explanation → prediction → application → critique) produces more flexible, generalizable knowledge; repeating an identical question teaches learners to memorize the answer verbatim and can create cue-dependency. alcoia's question-type rotation is its strongest lever for producing understanding rather than rote retention.
- **Timing and spacing matter, and immediate post-paragraph questions are the weakest form of retrieval.** An immediate question risks testing working memory rather than long-term memory; durable learning requires some delay and, ideally, spaced re-exposure. For a student cramming for tomorrow's exam, massed retrieval-with-feedback on triaged material is still the best use of limited time — but alcoia should be honest that it buys exam performance, not durable understanding.

## Key Findings

### 1. Retention vs. understanding are related but dissociable constructs
Kintsch's construction–integration model distinguishes the **textbase** (propositions extracted directly from the text) from the **situation model** (an integrated mental model built by combining the text with prior knowledge). Reproduction of a text draws on the textbase; genuine understanding requires a situation model. Kintsch's (1994, *American Psychologist*) central warning: "the problem for learning from text is that reading can be too easy… All too often, however, that understanding is only superficial," and "a feeling of understanding at the level of the textbase can conceal incomplete understanding at the level of the situation model." Memory for a text and learning from a text are dissociable — Moravcsik & Kintsch showed one can remember a text well without being able to use its information productively. This is the theoretical core of alcoia's problem: comprehension breakdown is a *situation-model* failure, and not every intervention that improves text memory improves the situation model.

### 2. Retrieval practice improves transfer — moderately, and conditionally
- **Overall testing effect**: Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017, *Review of Educational Research* 87(3):659–701) synthesized 272 independent effect sizes from 118 articles (M = 0.74, SD = 0.74), concluding "practice tests are more beneficial for learning than restudying and all other comparison conditions." Against restudy specifically the weighted mean was g ≈ +0.51; against filler/no-activity controls g ≈ +0.93. Multiple-choice practice tests (g ≈ +0.70) outperformed short-answer (g ≈ +0.48). Rowland (2014, *Psychological Bulletin*, 159 studies) independently reported g ≈ 0.50 with ~81% of comparisons favoring retrieval.
- **Transfer specifically**: Pan & Rickard (2018, *Psychological Bulletin* 144(7):710–756), the definitive transfer meta-analysis (192 effect sizes, 122 experiments, N ≈ 10,400): grand mean transfer d = 0.40 (95% CI [0.31, 0.50]). Transfer is largest across test formats (d ≈ 0.58) and to application/inference questions, and weaker for rearranged stimulus-response pairs and untested-but-related material. Critically, the authors caution that with publication-bias correction (PET-PEESE) and no facilitating moderators present, transfer can drop to near zero.
- **Butler (2010, *JEP:LMC* 36(5):1118–1133)**: repeated testing produced superior transfer vs. repeated studying — to new inferential questions within a domain (Exp. 1b, 2) and across domains (Exp. 3) — evidence that retrieval supports flexible knowledge, not just verbatim retention.
- **Karpicke & Blunt (2011, *Science* 331:772–775)**: retrieval practice produced more learning than elaborative concept mapping, including on inference questions and even when the final test was itself a concept map. This was contested (Mintzes et al. comment on methodological/epistemological grounds; a later replication found the advantage smaller for students already experienced with concept mapping).

### 3. The mechanism debate bears on whether comprehension improves
Two accounts compete: the **elaborative retrieval hypothesis** (Carpenter 2009 — retrieval generates semantically related information/mediators that aid later access) and the **episodic context account** (Karpicke, Lehman & Aue 2014 — retrieval reinstates and updates episodic context, narrowing the memory search set). Recent dissociation studies (Lehman & Karpicke; the *Journal of Psychiatric Research* subsequent-memory study) favor the episodic context account. This matters for alcoia: the dominant mechanism is about improving *access* to what is already stored, not about *constructing new meaning*. That is consistent with retrieval strengthening a situation model only when the elements of that model already exist to be retrieved.

### 4. The critical boundary — retrieval fails when comprehension/encoding failed
This is the central design constraint. The **bifurcation model** (Kornell, Bjork & Garcia 2011, *Journal of Memory and Language* 65(2):85–97) shows that testing *without feedback* splits ("bifurcates") the item-strength distribution: successfully retrieved items get a large boost; non-retrieved items get essentially nothing, while restudy strengthens all items modestly. Empirically:
- **Rowland (2014)**: with no feedback and retrieval success ≤ 50%, there is essentially no testing effect.
- **Field replication (Greving & Richter, university teaching)**: a testing effect occurred only for questions with retrievability between ~46–81%; "the testing effect can only occur when retrieval is successful, especially when no feedback is given."
- **Feedback rescues failure**: Kornell, Klein & Rawson (2015, *JEP:LMC* 41(2):283–294) found that when practice amount is held constant and feedback follows, retrieval *attempts* enhance learning regardless of whether the attempt succeeded — a failed attempt plus the correct answer is nearly as good as a success. A *Memory & Cognition* (2020) study confirmed retrieval success is the pivotal factor, with feedback protecting the effect. The **pretesting effect** (Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009; Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009) extends this: even *unsuccessful* guesses before study can enhance later learning — provided the correct answer is subsequently shown.
- **Prior-knowledge moderation is genuinely mixed.** Some studies find retrieval-practice benefits are largest for low-prior-knowledge/unfamiliar content and can equalize performance across knowledge levels (Cogliano et al. 2019, *Contemporary Educational Psychology* 56:76–90); others find prior knowledge moderates *elaboration* but not retrieval practice (Xiaofeng et al. 2016), or find no moderation at all (Buchin & Mulligan 2023). The defensible reading: prior *topic* knowledge does not strongly moderate the testing effect — a claim distinct from, and not to be conflated with, the robust *encoding/retrieval-success* prerequisite.
- **Complexity/element interactivity**: van Gog & Sweller (2015) argued the testing effect shrinks or vanishes as element interactivity rises; this was rebutted (Karpicke & Aue "The Testing Effect Is Alive and Well with Complex Materials"; Bedwell's Purdue dissertation showed retrieval benefits held for both intact and scrambled texts). Unresolved, but a genuine caution zone for very dense academic material.

**Implication**: When alcoia detects comprehension breakdown, retrieval alone is the *wrong* intervention if the reader never built even a textbase. The correct response is (a) always provide corrective feedback, and (b) if retrieval fails, drop to re-encoding scaffolds — re-read the specific passage, a bridging sub-question, or (for procedural content) a worked-example step — to lift retrievability above threshold *before* relying on unfeedbacked testing.

### 5. Do summaries build understanding? Mostly they build the illusion of it
Reading a fluent summary produces **cognitive ease**, readily mistaken for comprehension — the "illusion of comprehension"/"illusion of fluency" (Jacoby, Bjork & Kelley 1994; National Research Council 1994, *Learning, Remembering, Believing*, which discusses how "well-polished" lectures create the illusion, citing the "Feynman effect"). Re-reading builds recognition fluency, not recall, and learners systematically mistake familiarity for knowing (Koriat & Bjork). This is the empirical backbone of alcoia's "AI that makes you need AI less" philosophy: a NotebookLM-style summary is likely to *raise* confidence while leaving the situation model unbuilt. There is little evidence that reading a good summary alone builds durable understanding, and substantial evidence it inflates metacognitive judgments of learning.

### 6. Immediate vs. durable retrieval
Immediate, massed retrieval "requires little or no episodic context reinstatement and is certain to produce very poor long-term retention." Roediger & Karpicke (2006): restudy beats retrieval on an *immediate* test, but retrieval wins after 2-day and 1-week delays — the interaction is the entire point of the technique. Longer study-to-retrieval lags produce worse immediate but better delayed performance (Karpicke & Roediger 2007). Practically: a question asked within seconds of reading a paragraph largely taps working memory and constitutes a weak "desirable difficulty." Retrieval becomes learning-productive when it is effortful enough to require reconstruction from long-term memory — which needs a delay and/or intervening material. Note also the feedback-timing literature: delayed feedback (after a block or a day) often produces better long-term retention than immediate item-by-item feedback (Butler, Karpicke & Roediger 2007).

### 7. Spacing
Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006, *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3):354–380) reviewed 254 studies of distributed practice, finding an overall spacing benefit of roughly d ≈ 0.71 over massed practice. Cepeda et al. (2008, *Psychological Science* 19(11):1095–1102, N > 1,350) established the key non-monotonic rule: "The optimal gap increased as test delay increased. However, when measured as a proportion of test delay, the optimal gap declined from about 20 to 40% of a 1-week test delay to about 5 to 10% of a 1-year test delay." So for a 1-week horizon, a study gap of roughly 1–2 days is near-optimal; for a 1-month horizon, several days to a week. On expanding vs. equal-interval schedules, Karpicke & Roediger (2007) found equal (even) spacing with a *delayed first test* can beat expanding retrieval for long-term retention; the current consensus is that *any* spacing helps and the expanding-vs-equal difference is smaller and more context-dependent than once believed. Spacing effects for reading-comprehension/higher-order material are less studied than for vocabulary and paired associates. Within-session spacing helps somewhat; across-session (days) spacing helps far more for durability.

### 8. Varied vs. fixed retrieval — the most actionable lever
- Butler et al. (2017, geology concepts) and a 2022 analysis of 1,000+ students across two online courses found benefits were associated with completing *more distinct* practice activities rather than repeating the same one. The arXiv analysis "Beyond Repetition" concluded: "students experiencing repeated questions appear to memorize the answer verbatim… but are much less likely to engage in attempts to induce a general concept or skill than when experiencing different questions."
- PNAS (2024) "The role of variable retrieval in effective learning": varying retrieval cues/contexts improved later cue-free recall — and the benefit appeared specifically for retrieval practice, not restudy.
- Springer (2026) "Striking the Balance": "retrieval practice with varied questions led to superior transfer to novel problems compared with repeated practice with a single problem."
- **Transfer-appropriate processing / cue-dependency**: repeated identical questioning risks binding knowledge to a specific cue that fails to transfer; varying question type broadens retrieval routes into the situation model.
- **Interleaving** (Rohrer, Taylor, Kornell & Bjork): Rohrer et al. (2020 RCT) d = 0.83; Taylor & Rohrer (2010, 4th graders) 77% vs. 38% next-day (d ≈ 1.21); Rohrer et al. (2014) d ≈ 1.34 on delayed novel problems. Brunmair & Richter's meta-analysis found interleaving benefits are strong for some domains but small/inconsistent for interleaving mathematical *tasks* per se.
- **Question taxonomies**: Graesser & Person (1994, *American Educational Research Journal* 31(1):104–137) documented that classroom questioning is overwhelmingly shallow — "96% of questions that occur in the classroom come from the teacher and most of the questions are shallow," with roughly 3 questions asked per hour, and student questions "rarely high-level questions that involve inferences, multistep reasoning, the application of an idea to a new domain of knowledge, the synthesis of a new idea from multiple information sources, or the critical evaluation of a claim." Their shallow-vs-deep distinction ("Does the CPU use RAM?" vs. "How does the CPU use RAM?"), together with Bloom's revised taxonomy and the SOLO taxonomy, gives a principled scaffold for rotating question type by cognitive demand. There is direct evidence that varying question *type* (not just content) improves transfer.

### 9. Time-constrained learning / cramming
Cramming (massed practice) works for a next-day exam and fails afterward: it produces short-term recall sufficient to pass but collapses within a week. Kornell (2009, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 23(9):1297–1317, GRE-type word pairs): "Across experiments, spacing was more effective than massing for 90% of the participants, yet after the first study session, 72% of the participants believed that massing had been more effective than spacing" — a striking metacognitive illusion. Under severe time pressure the desirable difficulty of spacing becomes undesirable (you cannot space across days you don't have). The best use of one evening: rapid **retrieval practice with feedback on triaged, condensed material** (past questions, learning objectives), not re-reading; retrieval on a condensed summary beats reading the summary alone. Flashcard/spaced-repetition (Anki) evidence for exams is positive but modest and correlational: Deng, Gluckstein & Larsen (2015, *Perspectives on Medical Education* 4(6):308–313) found unique Anki cards reviewed independently predicted USMLE Step 1 score (B = 5.9 × 10⁻⁴, p = 0.024) — "Each additional 445 boards-style practice questions or 1700 unique Anki flashcards was associated with an additional point on Step 1 when controlling for other academic and psychological factors." Lu et al. (2021): Anki users 241.1 vs. non-users 235.5 (p = 0.012). Wothe et al. (2023): daily use correlated with higher Step 1 (median 238.0 vs. 233.5, p = 0.039) but not Step 2 CK. Some studies (Rocky Vista/IJMS; Spence 2025) found no significant benefit. All are observational and confounded by motivation.

### 10. Exam performance vs. genuine understanding
The education literature documents a real tension. "Teaching to the test" narrows curriculum, shifts class time to test-taking strategies, and is associated with shallower conceptual learning — effects concentrated in lower-SES schools (Shepard, "The Hazards of High-Stakes Testing"; McNeil's "double-entry bookkeeping"; Mary Lee Smith's ethnography). Self-determination theory work links controlling, high-stakes test conditions to reduced intrinsic motivation and less conceptual understanding. The goals-of-education debate spans knowledge transmission, skill/critical-thinking development, and credentialing; universities more often assess application and inference, while secondary schooling under high-stakes regimes tilts toward recognition and recall.

### 11. Subject-matter specificity
Retrieval practice is strongest for declarative and conceptual knowledge from text. For **procedural/problem-solving** knowledge (math), the evidence is weaker: multiple experiments (Frontiers in Psychology 2023, "Retrieval practice may not benefit mathematical word-problem solving") found retrieval practice on worked examples did *not* beat restudy of worked examples on delayed problem-solving. Cognitive load theory: for novices, worked examples beat unguided problem-solving (the worked-example effect), reversing only as expertise grows (expertise-reversal effect). A promising hybrid: a 2025 study found retrieval *prompts embedded in stepwise worked examples* did improve later recall and problem-solving. Yeo & Fazio (2019) found the optimal strategy depends on the goal — retrieval for remembering the text of a worked example; for learning a novel procedure, restudy can win at short retention intervals. **Implication**: alcoia should modulate question type by domain.

### 12. Metacognition and self-regulated learning
Comprehension monitoring is often inaccurate — adults are "remarkably unsuccessful at assessing how well they have comprehended a text." Diagnostic cues (ability to summarize the gist) predict comprehension; non-diagnostic cues (ease of reading, font size, interest) mislead (Thiede et al.; Koriat's cue-utilization framework; the Situation-Model Approach to Metacomprehension). Interventions that improve monitoring (concept-map/causal-diagram construction, self-explanation, iSTART's comprehension-monitoring/paraphrasing/prediction strategies) can improve both comprehension and metacognitive accuracy. Retrieval with feedback is itself a powerful metacognitive corrective: it converts a felt sense of knowing into a test of it. alcoia's capture of confidence at answer time is well-supported as a way to surface — and over time calibrate — the confidence-accuracy gap.

## Details / Interpretation
On the core question — *does retrieval produce understanding or only retention?* — the honest synthesis is: retrieval practice durably strengthens and makes more flexible whatever mental representation the learner has already constructed, and it modestly promotes inference and application (transfer d ≈ 0.40). It does *not* substitute for the initial construction of a situation model. For a reader whose comprehension is breaking down, retrieval is necessary but not sufficient: it strengthens a fragile textbase and can prompt the integration that builds a situation model *only if* the reader can retrieve and receives feedback. Where the reader never understood the passage, retrieval without feedback is the wrong intervention, and re-encoding scaffolds are required first. This is the pivot on which alcoia's effectiveness turns, because a comprehension-breakdown detector operates precisely in the low-retrieval-success regime where unfeedbacked testing is known to fail.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Always pair retrieval with feedback (highest priority; ship first).** The bifurcation model and its field replications make this non-negotiable: without feedback, questions only help items the reader already knows, and detected comprehension breakdown means low retrieval success. Show the correct answer (with a one-line pointer back to the source passage) after every attempt. *Threshold that would change this:* none — feedback is foundational.

**Stage 2 — Add a comprehension-repair fork keyed to retrieval success.** If a reader fails a retrieval question (or self-reports very low confidence and is wrong), do NOT escalate to another retrieval question. Route instead to a re-encoding scaffold: re-surface the specific passage, offer a bridging/scaffolding sub-question, or (for procedural content) a worked-example-completion step. Return to free retrieval only once the reader can succeed. *Threshold:* if per-session per-item retrieval success falls below ~50%, assume the situation model isn't built and prioritize re-encoding + feedback over further testing.

**Stage 3 — Rotate question TYPE, not just content (biggest lever toward understanding).** Build a per-passage pool spanning a taxonomy: definition → explanation ("why/how") → prediction → comparison → application → critique → integration. Never repeat an identical question; vary the angle on the same underlying knowledge. This is the best-supported route from retention toward flexible, transferable understanding, and it directly attacks verbatim-answer memorization and cue-dependency. Weight toward deep questions (Graesser "how/why"; Bloom apply/analyze/evaluate; SOLO relational/extended-abstract), which humans and textbooks systematically under-produce.

**Stage 4 — Introduce delay and spacing.** Prefer asking about a paragraph after the reader has continued a little further (a short lag) rather than within seconds — an immediate question mostly taps working memory. Where the model allows, re-surface a small number of previously-read key ideas later in the session, and (for returning learners) across days at a gap of roughly 20–40% of a one-week horizon (i.e., ~1–2 days), lengthening for longer horizons. Privacy-preserving spacing is achievable with **local-only or session-scoped storage** of item states (a device-local SM-2/FSRS-style scheduler), so no server-side cross-session learner tracking is required; across-session spacing can be opt-in and device-local.

**Stage 5 — Offer an explicit, honestly-labeled "exam-tomorrow" mode.** When a learner is time-constrained, switch to rapid retrieval-with-feedback on triaged/condensed material (favor recall over re-reading). Be transparent that this optimizes next-day exam performance, not durable understanding — cramming demonstrably buys the former and forfeits the latter, and learners reliably misjudge this trade-off.

**Stage 6 — Modulate by subject.** For mechanism-heavy sciences and argumentative humanities, favor deep explanation/application/critique questions. For mathematics/procedural passages, favor step-prediction and worked-example-completion prompts over free recall, and serve worked examples to novices before demanding retrieval.

**Stage 7 — Use confidence capture for metacognitive calibration.** Report the confidence-accuracy gap back to the learner ("you were confident but incorrect here") to puncture the illusion of comprehension, and aggregate anonymized calibration data for instructors. This turns alcoia's existing confidence-at-answer-time feature into a self-regulated-learning intervention, not merely telemetry.

**Instructor aggregate data.** Report both accuracy and calibration (confidence vs. correctness) by passage/concept, and flag concepts with *high confidence + low accuracy* (a collective illusion of comprehension) as the highest-value targets for re-teaching — these are exactly the situation-model failures that fluent summaries would mask.

## Caveats
- **Effect sizes are averages with wide dispersion.** Transfer d = 0.40 shrinks toward zero under publication-bias correction when no facilitating moderators are present (Pan & Rickard 2018); the testing effect is robust, but its transfer to genuinely novel understanding is the most contested part of the literature.
- **The retrieval-success prerequisite is the strongest boundary condition** and is precisely where a comprehension-breakdown tool operates. Deploying retrieval without feedback on not-yet-understood material would make alcoia counterproductive.
- **Prior-knowledge moderation is genuinely unresolved.** Do not overclaim that retrieval "works best for novices" or "requires expertise." The defensible claim is narrower: retrieval helps once the item can be retrieved, and feedback closes the gap when it cannot.
- **The complexity/element-interactivity debate (van Gog & Sweller vs. Karpicke/Aue) is unresolved**; very dense academic papers are a real risk zone.
- **Procedural/math domains are a documented weak spot** for retrieval practice; do not assume text-based findings transfer to problem-solving.
- **Anki/exam evidence is correlational and confounded**; the "~1 point per 1,700 cards" figure is a single-study association (Deng 2015), and null results exist. USMLE Step 1 became pass/fail in January 2022, changing the outcome landscape for newer studies.
- **Reading-comprehension-specific spaced retrieval** (versus vocabulary/paired associates) is under-researched; much of the spacing and testing-effect base rests on simpler materials.
- Several commercial/blog sources encountered during research (e.g., "65% transfer improvement," "4x") make quantitative claims not traceable to peer-reviewed primary sources; these were excluded from the findings above.
