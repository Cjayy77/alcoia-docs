# alcoia: An Evidence Base for "AI That Makes You Need AI Less"

## TL;DR
- **The core idea is scientifically sound but requires disciplined execution.** Browser telemetry (dwell/reading-time, scroll dynamics, mouse/cursor behavior, re-reading, response latency) can flag *when and where* comprehension is degrading with useful-but-imperfect accuracy — Bixler & D'Mello's user-independent gaze-based mind-wandering detector reached 74% accuracy (kappa .45) against a 52% chance baseline — and the single most robust intervention to convert that signal into durable learning is retrieval practice (testing effect, g ≈ 0.50; up to d = 1.50 in Karpicke & Blunt 2011). The philosophy "make the student do the cognitive work" is directly validated by the desirable-difficulties, generation, and cognitive-offloading literatures.
- **The hardest and most important requirement — distinguishing confusion from cognitive overload from boredom/disengagement — is only partially solvable from browser signals alone.** These states demand opposite instructional responses (productive confusion should be *preserved* and resolved; overload should be *reduced*; boredom should be *re-engaged*). alcoia should treat state classification as probabilistic, keep the student in the loop with quick self-reports, and default to low-cost retrieval prompts rather than confident automated diagnosis.
- **For instructors, the killer feature is not "who is struggling" but "where the class is confidently wrong."** A passage students find *hard* (low confidence, low accuracy) and a passage students are *confidently wrong* about (high confidence, low accuracy — the misconception signal) require opposite teaching responses. Aggregate, anonymized, confidence-weighted comprehension maps tied to specific passages are the most actionable and the least corrosive to student trust; individual surveillance risks undermining the intrinsic motivation the product exists to build.

## Key Findings

### 1. Behavioral detection of comprehension breakdown
- Eye-movement research (the Rayner tradition; see Rayner, Pollatsek, Ashby & Clifton) establishes that comprehension difficulty produces **longer fixations, shorter saccades, and more regressions (backward saccades)**. Regressions normally comprise ~10–15% of saccades in reading; conceptual and discourse-level difficulty reliably increase both the number and duration of fixations and the proportion of regressive saccades. Higher-order comprehension processes can override lexically-driven eye control and produce longer fixations or regressions back to earlier text (Rayner et al. 2006).
- **Not all regressions mean the same thing.** Research (Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 2016, "Regressions during reading: The cost depends on the cause") distinguishes regressions made for low-level oculomotor correction from those made to repair a comprehension breakdown — only the latter are diagnostic of understanding failure, and they show different inhibition-of-return signatures. Implication: raw "backtracking" counts will be noisy without contextual modeling.
- **Mind wandering has a measurable signature:** fewer and longer fixations, narrower/less-horizontal saccade patterns (the normal left-to-right reading pattern breaks down), higher blink rate (Smilek et al. 2010), and longer response initiation/completion times. Bixler & D'Mello's user-independent, self-caught detector reached **74% accuracy (kappa ≈ .45)** vs. 52% chance; multimodal (gaze + electrodermal) detectors reach **F1 ≈ 0.83** (Random Forest; eye-only 0.80, EDA-only 0.78).
- **Crucially for a hardware-free product: mouse and scroll behavior are usable proxies.** The "Wandering minds, wandering mice" study (*Computers in Human Behavior* 2020) showed computer-mouse tracking predicts mind wandering — when wandering, users took longer to initiate and complete responses and flipped the cursor more often along the x- and y-axes. Separate web-analytics work shows mouse-cursor position correlates with eye-gaze position, so cursor and scroll telemetry can partially substitute for eye tracking.
- **The best browser-only signal set:** reading time per passage vs. expected time for its difficulty; scroll velocity/regularity and scroll-back events (re-reading proxy); cursor movement kinematics; dwell time; re-reading loops; response latency to prompts; and tab-switching/idle as disengagement markers. Mills, Gregg, Bixler & D'Mello's "Eye-Mind reader" (2021) is the closest prior art: an interface that detects mind wandering during reading and responds to promote long-term comprehension.

### 2. The critical distinction: confusion vs. overload vs. boredom vs. disengagement
- **Confusion can be productive.** D'Mello, Lehman, Pekrun & Graesser (2014), "Confusion can be beneficial for learning" (*Learning and Instruction*, 29, 153–170), showed experimentally induced confusion (via contradictions/false feedback in agent trialogues) *improved* learning when appropriately induced, regulated, and resolved. This is Graesser's **"zone of optimal confusion."**
- **But unresolved confusion decays** into the zone of sub-optimal confusion → frustration → boredom → disengagement (D'Mello & Graesser affect-dynamics/transition model, 2012/2014). The instructional implication is that alcoia must not "rescue" a productively confused student too early — doing so removes the desirable difficulty and can prevent conceptual change.
- **Cognitive overload is different from confusion and needs the opposite response.** Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller 1988, 2010) separates **intrinsic** load (inherent element interactivity × prior knowledge), **extraneous** load (poor presentation, reducible by design), and **germane** load (working-memory resources devoted to schema building). Overload = working memory exceeded; the fix is to *reduce* load (segment, worked examples, strip extraneous material). Confusion = cognitive disequilibrium that should be *worked through*. The **expertise-reversal effect** warns that scaffolding helpful to novices harms experts, so responses must be prior-knowledge-sensitive.
- **Behavioral separation is hard.** Both confusion and overload can produce slow reading, regressions, and long dwell. The most reliable disambiguator available to a browser tool is a lightweight probe — a retrieval question or a one-tap "confused / too much at once / bored" self-report — plus context (is the passage high element-interactivity? is this student a novice?). Automated affect detection from behavior alone is not yet reliable enough to drive confident, differentiated interventions.

### 3. What actually builds comprehension and retention
- **Retrieval practice / testing effect is the anchor intervention.** Roediger & Karpicke (2006, *Psychological Science*, "Test-Enhanced Learning"), Experiment 2: the repeated-testing group recalled **61% of idea units after one week vs. 40%** for the repeated-study (reread) group — even though immediately (5 min) the pattern reversed (83% study vs. 71% testing), a vivid demonstration of the fluency trap. Meta-analyses: Rowland (2014, *Psychological Bulletin*) g ≈ 0.50; classroom meta-analysis Schwieren et al. (2017) d ≈ 0.56; Karpicke & Blunt (2011) reported d up to 1.50. Testing beats restudying even when time is matched. **This is the empirical heart of alcoia's "ask, don't tell" design.**
- **Testing effect shrinks as material complexity rises** — a caution: for very high-element-interactivity material, simple factual retrieval may underperform, and questions must be designed to match the complexity.
- **Spacing / distributed practice:** Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006, *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3):354–380) synthesized **839 assessments of distributed practice across 317 experiments in 184 articles**; spaced beats massed, and the optimal inter-study gap scales with the retention interval (~10–20% of it for multi-week retention, falling toward ~5% at one year; Cepeda et al. 2008). Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013) rank **practice testing and distributed practice as the ONLY two "high utility" techniques** of ten evaluated; rereading, highlighting, summarization, and keyword mnemonics are "low utility."
- **Desirable difficulties and the fluency illusion** (Bjork 1994; Bjork & Bjork 2011): conditions that slow acquisition (spacing, interleaving, retrieval, generation, varied practice) improve long-term retention. Students misjudge learning by *current processing fluency* — the fluency illusion — which is precisely why they over-rate rereading/highlighting and AI answers that "feel" clarifying. Storage strength ≠ retrieval strength.
- **Pretesting / prequestions** (Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009): attempting an answer *before* reading improves later retention *even when the guess is wrong*, provided corrective feedback follows. Pan & Sana showed prequestions before video reduce mind wandering — directly relevant to alcoia's design.
- **Self-explanation** (Chi et al. 1989/1994): d ≈ 0.61 (Fiorella & Mayer 2015 review); meta-analytic g ≈ 0.55–0.66 (Bisra et al. 2018, 69 effect sizes). Prompting "why?" and "how does this connect?" produces inference generation and transfer.
- **Hypercorrection effect** (Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001; Metcalfe & Finn 2011): high-confidence errors are corrected *more* readily than low-confidence errors, because surprising feedback captures attention (Butterfield & Mangels found the P300 ERP graded by confidence; Fazio & Marsh 2009 showed surprising feedback is better remembered). This is a gift to alcoia: eliciting a *confident* answer, then correcting it, maximizes durable correction — and the confidence data feeds the instructor dashboard. The effect persists over a week if a test intervenes after feedback.
- **Prediction error, surprise, and memory** (Event Segmentation Theory, Zacks et al. 2007): event boundaries occur at transient spikes in prediction error, opening an attentional gate that drives encoding. This helps explain why narratives/movies are remembered better than lectures, and suggests alcoia should exploit expectation violation (predict-then-reveal) as an encoding lever.
- **Impasse-driven learning** (VanLehn, Siler, Murray, Yamauchi & Baggett 2003; ~125 hours of physics tutoring analyzed): learning was uncommon *unless* the student reached an impasse; tutorial explanations only helped once an impasse existed. This validates letting students hit productive difficulty before intervening — the opposite of pre-emptive AI answering.

### 4. Instructor-facing analytics and what is actionable
- **The "taught ≠ learned" gap is real and large.** Mazur's use of the Force Concept Inventory (Hestenes, Wells & Swackhamer 1992) revealed Harvard physics students held the same Newtonian misconceptions after instruction. Hake's landmark survey (1998, *Am. J. Physics*, **N = 6,542 across 62 courses**) found 14 traditional courses averaged a normalized FCI gain of ⟨g⟩ = 0.23, while 48 interactive-engagement courses averaged ⟨g⟩ = 0.48 — "almost two standard deviations" higher. Crouch & Mazur report peer-instruction normalized gains of ~49–74%. The lesson: instructors systematically overestimate what students understood.
- **Hard vs. confidently-wrong is the decisive dashboard distinction.** A passage with low confidence + low accuracy signals difficulty (reteach/scaffold). A passage with high confidence + low accuracy signals a *shared misconception* (requires refutation/conceptual-change teaching, not repetition). These are opposite responses, and the hypercorrection literature says the confidently-wrong case is the highest-leverage teaching moment.
- **Dashboards frequently fail to change teaching.** Systematic reviews are blunt on this: Schwendimann et al. (2017, *IEEE TLT* 10(1):30–41; 55 of 346 papers) found the field dominated by exploratory proof-of-concept work with little longitudinal, authentic-setting evidence. Matcha et al. (2020, *IEEE TLT* 13(2):226–245) found dashboards largely **not grounded in learning theory** — visualizing achievement/comparison indicators without supporting sense-making or action. Jivet et al. (2017, EC-TEL, "Awareness is not enough") found dashboards create awareness but fail to translate it into regulatory action; one review corpus found only five teacher-dashboard studies explicitly claimed theoretical grounding. Actionability requires: **co-design with teachers** (Holstein, McLaren & Aleven), tying each metric to a concrete next action, teacher data literacy, and contextualization rather than raw indicators. Curran et al. (2024, *Review of Educational Research*) warn evidence on dashboard *impacts* is "particularly limited," with real risk of unintended negative consequences and lower-than-desired educator uptake.
- **Aggregate/anonymous beats individual surveillance — for both trust and efficacy.** Self-Determination Theory and Cognitive Evaluation Theory (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, meta-analysis of 128 experiments; Lepper & Greene 1975 showing surveillance specifically undermines intrinsic motivation) establish that being watched shifts perceived locus of causality to external and erodes intrinsic motivation and autonomy. Proctoring research (Woldeab & Brothen 2019, N=631) shows online monitoring depresses exam scores *specifically for high-test-anxiety students* (a person–situation interaction), while the temptation to cheat is unchanged. Surveillance studies document a "chilling effect" (self-censorship, panoptic self-regulation) and stress. Since alcoia's whole purpose is to build autonomous learners, individual monitoring is self-defeating; anonymized aggregates avoid the chilling effect while still informing teaching. Emerging SDT-informed dashboard design (e.g., *J. Computing in Higher Education* 2024) shows analytics can be pro-autonomy if built around support rather than control.

### 5. AI tutoring and what to avoid
- **AI help can produce worse learning — the "metacognitive laziness" risk.** Fan, Tang, Le, Shen, Tan, Zhao, Shen, Li & Gašević (2025, *British Journal of Educational Technology* 56(2):489–530, DOI 10.1111/bjet.13544) found that offloading to ChatGPT may promote dependence and trigger "metacognitive laziness": ChatGPT "can significantly improve short-term task performance, but it may not boost intrinsic motivation and knowledge gain and transfer." Gerlich (2025) and Zhai et al. (2024) link heavy generative-AI use to reduced critical-thinking dispositions via cognitive offloading. **This is the exact failure mode alcoia's philosophy is built to avoid.**
- **But well-designed AI can help — when it enforces effort.** Kestin, Miller, Klales, Milbourne & Ponti (2025, *Scientific Reports* 15:17458, DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6), an RCT in Harvard's Physical Sciences 2 (Fall 2023, N=194), found a research-based AI tutor produced more than twice the learning gains of an active-learning class *in less time* (median 49 min vs. ~60–75 min; z = −5.6, p < 10⁻⁸; effect sizes ~0.73–1.3 by quantile). Critically, that tutor was engineered around pedagogical best practices — active engagement, prompting, staged feedback, no answer-dumping — not around convenience.
- **Tutoring's real effect size is ~0.79, not 2.0.** Bloom's (1984) famous "2 sigma" claim (tutored students above 98% of controls) rests on a small 1980s study combining tutoring with strict mastery learning and an illustrative hand-drawn figure; it has not replicated at 2 SD. VanLehn (2011, *Educational Psychologist* 46(4), DOI 10.1080/00461520.2011.611369) found **human tutoring d ≈ 0.79 and intelligent tutoring systems d ≈ 0.76 — nearly equal**, with substep-based systems around 0.40. The realistic target for alcoia is a fraction of a standard deviation, achieved by triggering retrieval and impasse resolution, not by simulating a magical tutor.

## Details

**Browser-only signal engineering.** Because alcoia forgoes eye-tracking hardware, it must fuse weaker proxies. Evidence supports: (a) reading-time-vs-difficulty mismatch (dwell as a fixation proxy); (b) scroll dynamics — scroll-back and irregular scroll velocity as regression/re-reading proxies; (c) mouse-cursor kinematics (axis flips, slowed initiation) as mind-wandering markers; (d) response latency to embedded prompts; (e) idle/tab-switch as disengagement. None is individually decisive; a fused model plus occasional ground-truth probes (self-caught reports, embedded questions) is the validated approach (Bixler & D'Mello; Mills et al.'s "Eye-Mind reader," 2021). Expect modest accuracy (~70–80%), so design for graceful failure — a mistimed prompt should still be a useful retrieval opportunity, never a wrong "you don't understand" verdict.

**State-appropriate responses.** Map detected state → response. *Productive confusion*: do NOT answer; pose a Socratic/self-explanation prompt and let the student resolve it (impasse-driven learning). *Overload*: reduce load — segment the passage, offer a worked example, strip extraneous detail (CLT). *Boredom/disengagement*: re-engage with a prediction/prequestion or a surprising fact (prediction-error lever), or an attention cue. *Confident error*: elicit the answer, then deliver surprising corrective feedback (hypercorrection). Because behavior cannot cleanly separate these, gate the higher-stakes responses behind a one-tap self-report.

**Question quality.** Graesser & Person's taxonomy distinguishes shallow questions (yes/no, factual) from six deep-reasoning categories (causal antecedent, causal consequence, goal-orientation, enablement, instrumental/procedural, expectational), which correlate with Bloom levels 2–6 (r ≈ .64). Most student- and machine-generated questions cluster at Bloom's lowest two levels (~90% in some biology-course studies) — alcoia must deliberately generate deep "why/how/what-if" retrieval questions, not recall trivia, to build comprehension and transfer. Deep-question generation quality should be monitored and, if it drifts to Bloom level 1, gated behind review.

**Instructor dashboard design.** Surface, per passage/concept, aggregated and anonymized: (1) accuracy on retrieval questions; (2) mean confidence; (3) the confidence×accuracy quadrant (esp. high-confidence-wrong = misconception flag); (4) behavioral breakdown density (where reading slowed/regressed across the cohort); (5) resolution rate (did confusion resolve?). Each tile should link to a suggested action (reteach vs. refute vs. enrich). Avoid vanity metrics and individual identification. This mirrors what made peer instruction and just-in-time teaching powerful for Mazur: instructors could finally see, concretely, what students had *not* learned.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Build the retrieval engine first (highest evidence, lowest risk).** Ship embedded deep-reasoning retrieval questions and prequestions with corrective feedback and confidence capture, spaced over time. This alone delivers the best-supported gains (testing effect g ≈ 0.5+, spacing among Dunlosky's two "high-utility" techniques, hypercorrection) regardless of detection quality. *Benchmark:* delayed (1-week+) retention vs. a reread control — not in-session performance.

**Stage 2 — Layer in behavioral detection as a *trigger*, not a diagnosis.** Use fused browser telemetry to decide *when* to prompt, accepting ~70–80% accuracy. Never let the model silently decide a student "doesn't understand"; keep a one-tap self-report to disambiguate confusion/overload/boredom. *Benchmark to advance:* prompts feel well-timed to users >70% of the time in testing.

**Stage 3 — Ship the instructor dashboard as anonymized, confidence-weighted concept maps.** Prioritize the confidently-wrong (misconception) view and per-passage breakdown density, each tied to a concrete pedagogical action. Co-design tiles with real instructors before launch (dashboards without co-design and theory grounding are routinely ignored — Schwendimann, Matcha, Jivet). *Benchmark:* instructors take a documented instructional action after ≥50% of dashboard sessions.

**Guardrails that define the product.**
- **Never answer for the student when confusion is productive.** Default to Socratic prompts and self-explanation; only reduce load when *overload* (not confusion) is indicated. This is the operational meaning of "AI that makes you need AI less," and it is what separated the successful Kestin tutor from the metacognitive-laziness failure mode in Fan et al.
- **Keep instructor data aggregate and anonymous.** Individual surveillance would undermine the intrinsic motivation and autonomy the product exists to build (SDT; Deci/Ryan; Lepper & Greene) and depress performance for anxious students (Woldeab & Brothen).
- **Exploit surprise and prediction error** (predict-then-reveal, confident-error correction) to strengthen encoding.
- **Measure yourself on delayed retention and transfer, not on in-session fluency or satisfaction** — the fluency illusion means students will *prefer* the tool that teaches them least.

**Thresholds that would change the plan.** If behavioral detection cannot exceed chance-adjusted kappa ~0.3 in your population, drop automated triggering and rely on fixed spaced prompts + self-report. If deep-question generation drifts to Bloom level 1, gate questions behind human/instructor review. If dashboard co-design shows instructors won't act on aggregate data, pivot to student-facing self-regulation feedback (still evidence-supported).

## Caveats
- **Detection accuracy is bounded.** Nearly all cited mind-wandering/comprehension detectors were built with eye-tracking or physiological sensors; browser-only proxies will be weaker, and much of the mouse/scroll evidence comes from adjacent tasks (search abandonment, web analytics, an online problem-solving task) rather than validated *comprehension* detection. Treat any single-signal claim as provisional.
- **Confusion vs. overload cannot be reliably separated from behavior alone** with current evidence; the self-report loop is not optional.
- **Effect sizes are context-dependent.** The testing effect shrinks with material complexity; optimal spacing intervals must be tuned to the retention target; peer-instruction and AI-tutor gains depend heavily on implementation quality (Kestin's result used a carefully engineered tutor and may not generalize to an off-the-shelf LLM). Recent evidence also shows peer instruction can *disadvantage* lower-preparation students in heterogeneous classes.
- **The 2-sigma benchmark is a myth for planning purposes.** Realistic tutoring effects are ~0.4–0.8 SD (VanLehn 2011); set expectations accordingly.
- **Dashboard evidence is thin.** The learning-analytics field has limited empirical evidence that dashboards actually change teaching; alcoia's instructor value proposition is plausible and theory-aligned but must be validated, not assumed.
- **Generative-AI-in-education evidence (2024–2026) is fast-moving and mixed,** with several findings drawn from preprints and single-institution studies; the cognitive-offloading and metacognitive-laziness risks are well-attested directionally, but exact magnitudes remain uncertain.
